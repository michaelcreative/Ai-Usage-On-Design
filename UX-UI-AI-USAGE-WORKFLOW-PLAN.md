# UX/UI Designer Workflow × AI Usage Plan

> **Theme:** Simplify to Amplify  
> **Purpose:** Streamline and standardise design delivery, strengthen collaboration, and help designers use AI with sound judgement—not simply use more tools.  
> **Audience:** UX/UI designers, UX researchers, UX writers, design leads, product partners, and delivery teams.  
> **Status:** Working plan — v1.0, August 2026

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This plan defines a practical way for UX/UI designers to work smarter with AI across the full product design lifecycle. It brings together three learning pillars:

1. **How we align and deliver** — AI-enabled workflows, repeatable prompts, templates, and quality checks.
2. **How we think** — leadership principles, critical thinking, responsible judgement, and decision clarity.
3. **How we show up** — communication, stakeholder management, feedback, facilitation, and other soft skills.

The desired outcome is a design practice that is faster and more consistent while remaining human-led, evidence-based, secure, accessible, and aligned with the HASE brand.

AI is treated as a **co-pilot**. It can accelerate exploration, synthesis, drafting, comparison, documentation, and quality assurance. Designers remain accountable for the problem definition, evidence, decisions, customer impact, regulatory interpretation, and final approval.

## 2. North Star: Simplify to Amplify

### Vision

Streamline and standardise design delivery to improve efficiency and consistency, while strengthening scalable and sustainable collaboration across project teams and design sub-teams.

### What success looks like

- A designer can quickly decide **which method, template, component, or AI tool to use**.
- Repeated work starts from a recommended default rather than a blank page.
- AI outputs are traceable, challenged, validated, and refined before use.
- Design assets, decisions, prompts, research evidence, and delivery records are easy to find.
- Review cycles focus on meaningful product decisions instead of preventable ambiguity.
- The visual ecosystem remains premium, unified, accessible, and technically feasible.

### Working principles

| Principle | Meaning in daily design work |
|---|---|
| Human-led, AI-assisted | AI proposes or accelerates; people frame, judge, decide, and approve. |
| Evidence before confidence | Do not treat a fluent AI answer as evidence. Check it against research, analytics, policies, and source material. |
| Standardise the repeatable | Standardise prompts, templates, naming, components, review criteria, and handoffs. |
| Keep judgement visible | Record assumptions, alternatives, evidence, risks, and decisions. |
| Reuse before recreating | Search the approved library and existing work before generating new assets. |
| Safe by design | Do not enter customer, confidential, restricted, or unapproved banking data into public AI tools. |
| Accessible by default | Include accessibility and inclusive-design checks from the start, not only at handoff. |
| Measure and improve | Observe real outcomes, document learning, and feed improvements back into the workflow. |

## 4. Standard UX/UI Workflow with AI

The standard lifecycle is:

**Discover → Research → Prototype → Pilot → Production → Observe → Optimize → Scale**

Each stage has a human decision owner, approved AI support, an evidence requirement, and an exit check.

### Workflow map

| Stage | Design purpose | AI can assist with | Human responsibility | Key deliverables | Exit criteria |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Discover** | Align on the business request, user problem, constraints, risks, and success measures. | Turn a brief into questions; identify gaps; draft stakeholder maps; prepare workshop plans; summarise approved documents. | Challenge the request; define the real problem; confirm compliance and technical constraints; align stakeholders. | Design brief, problem statement, stakeholder map, assumptions log, KPI hypothesis | Problem, audience, scope, owner, constraints, and success measures are agreed. |
| **2. Research** | Build evidence about users, journeys, behaviours, competitors, and opportunities. | Draft research plans and discussion guides; organise notes; cluster observations; compare competitors; suggest hypotheses. | Obtain consent; conduct research; protect participant data; verify themes against raw evidence; distinguish fact from interpretation. | Research plan, interview guide, competitor audit, journey map, insights, opportunity areas | Insights are traceable to sources and sufficiently strong to guide design. |
| **3. Prototype** | Explore and communicate possible solutions quickly. | Generate alternative flows; critique information hierarchy; draft UX copy variants; create edge-case lists; support early wireframes. | Select concepts; protect brand integrity; apply design-system rules; resolve trade-offs; ensure feasibility and accessibility. | User flow, wireframes, content draft, interaction model, clickable prototype | The concept addresses the validated problem and is ready to test. |
| **4. Pilot** | Test the proposed experience with users and partners before full delivery. | Draft test tasks; identify likely usability risks; organise observations; calculate simple results; compare iterations. | Moderate sessions; interpret behaviour; prioritise findings; decide what changes; document unresolved risks. | Test plan, usability findings, issue severity, iteration log, pilot recommendation | Critical issues are resolved or accepted by an accountable owner. |
| **5. Production** | Turn the validated solution into build-ready design. | Draft annotations; generate state and content inventories; check consistency; prepare handoff summaries; identify missing states. | Finalise responsive behaviour, accessibility, components, tokens, content, error states, analytics, and specifications. | Hi-fi design, component mapping, design specs, content matrix, tracking plan, QA checklist | Design is approved, complete, traceable, feasible, and ready for implementation. |
| **6. Observe** | Understand how the released experience performs in real use. | Summarise analytics and session patterns; cluster feedback; flag anomalies; prepare monitoring reports. | Validate data definitions; protect privacy; separate correlation from causation; connect behaviour with qualitative evidence. | KPI dashboard, feedback log, session insights, issue list, post-launch review | Performance and customer signals are understood with known limitations. |
| **7. Optimize** | Improve the experience based on validated evidence. | Generate test hypotheses; compare options; draft experiment plans; update documentation. | Prioritise opportunities; assess risk; approve experiments; interpret results; prevent local optimisation that harms the journey. | Prioritised backlog, experiment brief, revised design, decision log | Improvement is validated and documented, or the hypothesis is rejected with learning captured. |
| **8. Scale** | Convert proven learning into reusable standards, assets, and team capability. | Convert patterns into templates; draft guidance; identify reuse candidates; maintain prompt and knowledge libraries. | Approve standards; govern the library; train the team; manage versions, exceptions, and adoption. | Reusable components, playbooks, prompt patterns, case studies, training material | The solution can be reused safely and consistently with clear ownership. |

## 5. Human–AI Operating Model

### Task ownership labels

Use one label when planning an AI-assisted task:

| Label | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **H — Human-led** | A person performs and owns the task; AI is not required. | Stakeholder negotiation, user interview moderation, final design approval |
| **A — AI-assisted** | A person owns the task and uses AI to accelerate part of it. | Drafting a research guide, clustering notes, producing copy alternatives |
| **AI — AI-executed, human-reviewed** | AI produces a bounded output using approved inputs; a person verifies it before use. | Formatting a state inventory, comparing approved source documents |
| **NA — No AI** | AI use is prohibited because of sensitivity, policy, or risk. | Unapproved customer data, credentials, confidential strategy, restricted banking material |

### The core AI loop

1. **Frame** — define the problem, audience, context, constraints, output, and success criteria.
2. **Select** — choose the smallest suitable approved tool or combination of tools.
3. **Generate** — request structured alternatives or analysis, not a single unquestioned answer.
4. **Challenge** — ask what may be wrong, missing, biased, unsupported, or risky.
5. **Verify** — check against primary sources, raw research, analytics, brand rules, accessibility, policy, and engineering constraints.
6. **Decide** — a named human owner accepts, rejects, or revises the output.
7. **Document** — capture the prompt, sources, decision, limitations, and reusable learning when material.
8. **Improve** — update the prompt, template, or workflow based on actual results.

## 6. Standard Prompt Formula

Use **BRIEF + CHECK** as the default prompting structure.

### BRIEF

| Element | What to provide |
|---|---|
| **B — Background** | Project, product, audience, stage, and why the task matters |
| **R — Role** | The relevant perspective AI should adopt, without treating that role as authority |
| **I — Instructions** | The exact task, steps, and boundaries |
| **E — Examples & evidence** | Approved references, examples, source material, and known facts |
| **F — Format** | Required structure, language, length, table columns, or file type |

### CHECK

| Element | Validation instruction |
|---|---|
| **C — Criteria** | State what a strong answer must achieve. |
| **H — Hallucination control** | Ask AI to label assumptions, unknowns, and unsupported claims. |
| **E — Evaluate alternatives** | Request 2–3 options with trade-offs where a decision is involved. |
| **C — Cite sources** | Require links or exact source references for factual claims when available. |
| **K — Keep a human gate** | Specify which parts require human verification or approval. |

### Reusable prompt template

```text
Background:
[Project, audience, design stage, problem and reason]

Role:
[Relevant specialist perspective]

Instructions:
[Exact task and required steps]

Evidence / approved references:
[Source material and known facts]

Format:
[Output structure, language and length]

Quality criteria:
[What good looks like]

Validation:
- Separate facts, interpretations and assumptions.
- Identify missing information and potential bias.
- Do not invent user evidence, quotes, metrics, policies or sources.
- Provide alternatives and trade-offs where relevant.
- Mark items that require human, compliance, legal, accessibility or engineering review.
```

## 7. Tool Selection and AI Head-to-Head Method

Do not select a tool only because it is popular. Start with the task and risk.

### Selection criteria

| Criterion | Question |
|---|---|
| Task fit | Is it designed for text, research, coding, images, prototyping, analysis, or workflow automation? |
| Output quality | Is the result accurate, relevant, controllable, and useful? |
| Evidence support | Can it work from supplied sources and show where claims came from? |
| Data handling | Is the tool approved for the information classification being used? |
| Design integration | Does it connect safely with Figma, documentation, analytics, or development workflows? |
| Control | Can users edit, constrain, version, and reproduce the output? |
| Collaboration | Can the team review, comment, share, and govern the work? |
| Accessibility | Does the output support inclusive and accessible design? |
| Cost and limits | Are subscription features, usage caps, export options, and ownership acceptable? |
| Learning curve | Can designers use it reliably after reasonable training? |

### Head-to-head test protocol

1. Use the **same task, prompt, inputs, and time box** for each tool.
2. Remove confidential and personally identifiable information.
3. Score outputs independently before discussing preferences.
4. Use a 1–5 scale for task fit, accuracy, controllability, evidence, speed, integration, safety, and value.
5. Record failure modes, not only the best-looking output.
6. State whether the comparison uses free or paid features.
7. Recommend a tool **for a task**, not one universal winner.

### Example scorecard

| Tool | Task fit | Accuracy | Control | Evidence | Speed | Integration | Safety | Value | Key failure |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Tool A | /5 | /5 | /5 | /5 | /5 | /5 | /5 | /5 | To test |
| Tool B | /5 | /5 | /5 | /5 | /5 | /5 | /5 | /5 | To test |
| Tool C | /5 | /5 | /5 | /5 | /5 | /5 | /5 | /5 | To test |

## 8. Critical Thinking and Output Validation

### Never accept the first answer by default

Use the **TRACE** review:

| Check | Designer question |
|---|---|
| **T — Truth** | Which claims are facts, and what evidence supports them? |
| **R — Relevance** | Does the answer address the actual user and business problem? |
| **A — Assumptions** | What has been assumed, invented, simplified, or left unknown? |
| **C — Consequences** | Who could be excluded, misled, harmed, or burdened by this recommendation? |
| **E — Execution** | Is it compliant, accessible, feasible, measurable, and consistent with the design system? |

### Evidence hierarchy

Prefer evidence in this order where applicable:

1. Approved policy, regulatory, legal, security, and compliance sources.
2. Direct user research and verified product analytics.
3. Current design-system, brand, content, and engineering documentation.
4. Primary external sources and official product documentation.
5. Expert interpretation with its assumptions stated.
6. AI-generated suggestions as hypotheses—not evidence.

### Mandatory escalation

Pause and ask the accountable specialist when an output involves:

- regulatory, legal, compliance, financial, or security interpretation;
- customer eligibility, fees, rates, rewards, terms, or product claims;
- accessibility exceptions or potential exclusion;
- customer or employee personal data;
- unverified research quotes, fabricated metrics, or unclear source ownership;
- production code, analytics rules, or irreversible customer-facing changes.

## 9. Learning Initiatives and Deliverables

### #1 — AI Basic 101: Let's Learn AI Together

**Objective:** Build practical foundations for thinking with AI as a designer—effective prompting, fit-for-purpose tool selection, validation, and judgement.

| Module | Focus | Activities | Deliverable | Timing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **AI 101 — Mastering the Prompt** | Standard prompting formula | Introduce BRIEF + CHECK; before/after prompt exercises; team prompt clinic | Prompt guide, reusable templates, starter prompt library | July foundation; refresh in August |
| **AI 102 — AI Head-to-Heads** | Tool comparison and selection | Run controlled tests for visual and content-generation tasks; compare free and subscription tiers | Tool scorecard, pros/cons, task-based recommendations | September |
| **AI 103 — Critical Thinking Coach** | Challenge and improve AI output | Practise TRACE; find hallucinations, bias, weak evidence, and missing constraints; refine outputs | Validation checklist, red-team exercises, responsible-use guide | November |

**Clarification: “How to set AI” and “How to train AI”**

- **Set AI:** configure the context, instructions, approved knowledge, examples, output rules, privacy boundaries, and review criteria.
- **Improve AI for team use:** refine prompts, examples, reference libraries, evaluation cases, and feedback loops.
- **Model training or fine-tuning:** a separate technical and governance activity; it is not the starting requirement for normal design-team adoption.

### #2 — Leadership 101: Relationships and Communication

**Objective:** Translate the Leadership Guide into memorable daily actions and use clear narrative to introduce team changes.

**Proposed three essentials:**

1. **Build the relationship** — understand goals, pressures, incentives, preferences, and decision authority.
2. **Create shared clarity** — align on the problem, evidence, trade-offs, roles, and next decision.
3. **Follow through visibly** — document ownership, action, timing, and what changed.

**Real designer scenarios:**

- **Giving feedback:** describe the observed design issue, customer or delivery impact, evidence, and a concrete next step.
- **Gaining stakeholder buy-in:** connect the proposal to shared outcomes, show alternatives and trade-offs, and ask for a specific decision.
- **Resolving conflict:** separate people from the issue, restate common goals, surface constraints, and agree on the next test or decision owner.

**Deliverable:** one-page Leadership Quick Guide with a method/decision tree and 2–3 design scenarios.  
**Timing:** August onward.

### #3 — Soft Skills Workshop

**Objective:** Build observable interpersonal skills through practice, not lecture.

**Recommended first topic: Asking Better Questions for Stakeholder Clarity**

Observable skills:

1. State the core issue in one sentence.
2. Ask neutral, open questions before suggesting a solution.
3. Confirm context, impact, attempted actions, decision owner, and next step.

**One-page question framework — CLEAR**

| Element | Prompt |
|---|---|
| **C — Core issue** | What exactly needs attention or decision? |
| **L — Landscape** | What user, business, technical, policy, and timeline context is essential? |
| **E — Effect** | What is the current or expected impact? |
| **A — Attempts** | What has already been tried or ruled out, and why? |
| **R — Resolution** | What help, decision, owner, or next action is required? |

Suggested workshop format: 10-minute demonstration, 20-minute paired role-play, 15-minute feedback, 10-minute repeat, 5-minute commitment.  
**Deliverable:** one-page CLEAR guide, scenario cards, observer checklist.  
**Timing:** before the end of October.

### #4 — Improve Change Announcements

**Objective:** Make every new process announcement clear, memorable, and actionable.

#### Hook, Line, Sinker template

| Part | Purpose | Template |
|---|---|---|
| **Hook** | Name the pain in one relatable sentence. | “Tired of [specific repeated problem]?” |
| **Line** | Explain the change and why it helps. | “We are introducing [change] so that [team benefit].” |
| **Sinker** | Give one simple action with timing. | “By [date], please [specific action].” |

#### Example: AI-assisted documentation

- **Hook:** Tired of recreating project summaries and still missing important decisions?
- **Line:** We are introducing an approved AI-assisted documentation template so teams can capture context, evidence, risks, and ownership consistently.
- **Sinker:** From Monday, use the template for every new project kickoff and have the project owner review the summary before sharing.

#### Example: New design folder structure

- **Hook:** Spending too much time looking for the latest approved design?
- **Line:** We are introducing a standard folder and page structure so everyone can find work-in-progress, review, and approved designs quickly.
- **Sinker:** This week, save all new project work under `Project / 01 WIP / 02 Review / 03 Approved` and nominate one file owner.

**Deliverable:** announcement template, example library, and short team walkthrough.  
**Timing:** after September.

### Governance rules

- Assign an owner and review date to every guide and template.
- Mark content as **Draft**, **Approved**, **Deprecated**, or **Archived**.
- Store only sanitised examples; do not use live customer or restricted business data.
- Link to approved systems instead of duplicating sensitive documents.
- Record major changes in a changelog and announce them using Hook, Line, Sinker.
- Review recommended tools and policies at least quarterly or whenever risk guidance changes.
- Retire prompts or templates that repeatedly produce weak or unsafe results.

## 12. Measurement Plan

Measure both efficiency and quality. Faster output alone is not success.

| Outcome | Example measure | Baseline / target method |
|---|---|---|
| Adoption | % of designers using the standard workflow and templates | Baseline before launch; monthly pulse |
| Prompt quality | % of sampled prompts containing BRIEF + CHECK essentials | Monthly sample review |
| Validation behaviour | % of material AI outputs with sources, assumptions, and human approval recorded | Monthly audit |
| Delivery efficiency | Time from brief to testable prototype; handoff preparation time | Compare similar projects before and after pilot |
| Review efficiency | Number of avoidable review rounds or missing-state issues | Track in delivery retrospective |
| Design consistency | Reuse of approved components/patterns; design-system exceptions | Quarterly library review |
| Output quality | Usability issue severity, accessibility defects, content corrections, implementation rework | Compare pilot and control projects where possible |
| Confidence | Designer confidence in choosing, prompting, and challenging AI | Short pre/post module survey |
| Safety | Sensitive-data incidents, unsupported claims, or policy breaches | Target: zero; review every incident |
| Business/customer effect | Stage-specific KPI connected to the original problem | Defined during Discover; reviewed in Observe |

### Pilot success criteria

After the first two pilot projects, continue or scale only if:

- designers report that the workflow reduces ambiguity;
- evidence quality and human review do not decline;
- no prohibited data is used;
- delivery or review effort improves in a measurable way;
- outputs remain accessible, on-brand, and feasible;
- the team can identify where AI did **not** add value.

## 13. 30-Day Starting Plan

### Week 1 — Align

- Confirm sponsors, contributors, and approval partners.
- Select two low-risk, repeatable design use cases.
- Establish approved tools and prohibited data types.
- Capture current time, quality, and rework baselines.

### Week 2 — Standardise

- Pilot BRIEF + CHECK on real tasks.
- Create initial prompt and validation templates.
- Define naming, folder, ownership, and version rules.
- Prepare the AI 102 tool-comparison test cases.

### Week 3 — Test

- Run controlled head-to-head tests.
- Review results with design, research, content, engineering, and risk partners.
- Record weak outputs, bias, hallucinations, and integration constraints.

### Week 4 — Learn and decide

- Publish task-based recommendations and boundaries.
- Run a short practice session using TRACE.
- Measure time saved, rework, confidence, and quality.
- Decide what to adopt, revise, stop, or test next.

## 14. Definition of Done for AI-Assisted Design Work

Before an AI-assisted deliverable is shared or approved, confirm:

- [ ] The problem, audience, design stage, and intended decision are clear.
- [ ] The AI tool is approved for the task and data classification.
- [ ] No prohibited customer, employee, confidential, credential, or restricted data was entered.
- [ ] Facts, interpretations, assumptions, and unknowns are separated.
- [ ] Important factual claims are checked against current authoritative sources.
- [ ] Research insights and quotes are traceable to real evidence.
- [ ] Alternatives and trade-offs were considered where relevant.
- [ ] Brand, design-system, accessibility, content, and technical constraints were checked.
- [ ] Regulatory, legal, compliance, security, or financial claims received specialist review where required.
- [ ] A named human owner reviewed and accepted the final output.
- [ ] The decision and material limitations are documented.
- [ ] Reusable learning is added to the approved library without sensitive content.

## 16. Expected Outcomes

By the end of the initial programme, the team should have:

- one shared AI Guide for design;
- a standard prompt formula and reusable prompt library;
- a task-based AI tool scorecard covering free and subscription differences;
- a practical critical-thinking and validation checklist;
- a leadership quick guide focused on relationships, clarity, and follow-through;
- a soft-skills toolkit based on observable practice;
- a Hook, Line, Sinker announcement framework;
- a governed resource library with ownership and versioning;
- evidence showing whether AI improves delivery without reducing design quality or increasing risk.

The long-term goal is not to automate designers. It is to remove avoidable effort so designers can spend more time understanding people, making sound decisions, crafting coherent experiences, and building trust.
